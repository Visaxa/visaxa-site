The demonstration went well.

The appointment appeared in the calendar. The client received a confirmation. The presenter moved the booking, took a deposit, opened a report, and answered every question without losing the thread.

I could picture the same screen at reception.

Then I noticed who had done all the work.

The presenter knew which client record to open, which permission to use, where every control lived, and what would happen next. No phone rang halfway through. No professional needed the chair at the same time. No client chose the wrong service on a small phone screen. Nothing had to be corrected while another person waited.

The demo had shown that the product could perform the tasks.

It had not shown that our people could.

<KeyTakeaway>
Before signing, give representative receptionists, professionals, managers, and clients believable outcomes to achieve in the candidate system. Watch rather than guide. Record the correct result, time, errors, assistance, workarounds, device, permissions, and what changes after limited training. Decide by the consequence of each failure, not by a universal score. A short test reduces adoption uncertainty; it does not guarantee go-live success.
</KeyTakeaway>

## The demonstration had tested the wrong person

I first thought the answer was to ask for a longer demo.

That would have produced more features and more questions, but the same prepared person would still have been driving.

NIST's work on usability information for software purchasing describes the underlying procurement problem: buyers often have little visibility into how easy a product will be to learn, how productively their own users will work, or how much support they will need. A supplier may provide usability results, but a buyer still has to judge whether the users, tasks, conditions, and interventions are relevant—or replicate the test. ([NIST Industry Usability Report](https://www.nist.gov/itl/tted/iusr-project-industry-usability-report), page updated August 27, 2025; accessed November 11, 2026.)

The previous investigation into [what salon software costs at the next growth threshold](/blog/what-will-salon-software-cost-as-your-salon-grows) had left several benefits conditional on adoption. Reduced front-desk work, client self-service, fewer corrections, and usable professional schedules were not financial results merely because the functions existed.

Now I needed a different kind of evidence: ordinary people attempting ordinary work.

## I stopped asking people to follow clicks

My first test script was really another demonstration.

“Open the calendar, choose Add, select the client, then change the service.”

Anyone who followed the instructions would probably reach the end. I would learn whether they could follow my route, not whether they could find one.

Government usability guidance recommends giving participants a clear, believable goal without hinting at the answer, then mostly watching and listening. It also says the participants should be actual or likely users, and that the session is testing the service rather than judging the person. ([GOV.UK moderated usability testing](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/using-moderated-usability-testing), updated October 3, 2017; [Digital.gov usability-test guide](https://digital.gov/resources/how-conduct-usability-test), accessed November 11, 2026.)

So the instruction became:

> A regular client has called while you are handling another arrival. She needs next Tuesday's colour appointment moved to Thursday, but wants to keep the same professional. Update the booking and tell her what changed.

The goal was clear. The path was not supplied.

That small change exposed questions the feature list had hidden. Could reception find the correct client quickly? Did the service duration move with the booking? Was the professional still available? Could the receptionist see what had changed without manager access? Did the system make the final state clear enough to explain over the phone?

## “The staff” turned into four different users

I had been speaking about adoption as though the salon had one kind of user.

Reception works across people, services, payments, calls, interruptions, and corrections. A professional may need only today's schedule, a client note, a changed service, and a way to record completed work. A manager needs permission boundaries and exceptions. A client sees a booking journey on their own phone, without training.

One confident manager could not stand in for all four.

I wrote a small set of role-specific tasks instead.

**Reception**

- create a realistic multi-service appointment while responding to a second request;
- move the booking without losing the right professional or duration;
- correct a wrong service and identify what happens to a deposit;
- find the right client when two records look similar;
- recognise when manager approval is required.

**Professionals**

- find today's schedule on the device they would really use;
- notice a same-day change;
- review the information needed before the service;
- record completed work without asking reception to reconstruct it;
- find only the compensation or tip information their role should be allowed to see.

**Clients**

- find the appropriate service on a mobile screen;
- understand price, duration, professional, and availability;
- complete a booking and recognise that it succeeded;
- locate the confirmation;
- reschedule or cancel and recover from an input error.

**Owner or manager**

- resolve one permission boundary without taking over the participant's task;
- identify which workflows cannot be represented in the trial;
- distinguish a missing configuration from a missing capability.

These were not claims about how every salon works. They were candidate tasks. Each salon would need to replace them with its own required outcomes, roles, devices, policies, and common exceptions.

## The useful test looked like an ordinary Tuesday

The first clean booking still worked.

Then I added the parts the demo had removed.

The receptionist had to move an appointment while another request was waiting. A professional checked the schedule from a phone rather than the office computer. The client chose between two similarly named services. A deposit already existed. A correction needed manager permission. One booking had to be handed from reception to the professional without a private message or paper note.

GOV.UK's quality guidance makes the general point that a service should be tested for usability across devices and under both normal and unusual conditions. That does not define a salon test for us. It does explain why one ideal path is weak evidence. ([GOV.UK quality assurance guidance](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/technology/quality-assurance-testing-your-service-regularly), updated June 28, 2017; accessed November 11, 2026.)

I did not need to manufacture chaos. I needed to preserve enough reality for the handoffs and exceptions to remain visible.

## A completed task could still hide the problem

At first I wrote only pass or fail.

That was too crude.

To see what the sheet needed to distinguish, I sketched four hypothetical results. One receptionist might complete the task after a hint. Another might reach the right final appointment but briefly place it with the wrong professional. A client might believe the cancellation was complete while it was still waiting for confirmation. A professional might finish quickly by writing the missing detail in a separate message.

All four could be marked “completed.” They would not represent the same evidence.

NIST usability guidance separates effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction, and lists task success, time, errors, assistance, and learnability as distinct observations. The source concerns health-record systems, not salons, so it does not give us a salon pass mark. Its useful lesson is narrower: record the signals separately before interpreting them. ([NISTIR 7741](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/IR/nistir7741.pdf), 2010, pp. 50–51; accessed November 11, 2026.)

My observation sheet became:

| Record | What I needed to see |
| --- | --- |
| Outcome | Was the final appointment, client action, payment state, or note correct? |
| Time | How long did the task take under the stated conditions? |
| Errors | What incorrect action or state occurred before completion? |
| Assistance | Did the observer prompt, instruct, or take over? |
| Workaround | Did the participant create a message, paper note, spreadsheet, or memory task outside the system? |
| Confidence | Did the person know the task was complete and correct? |
| Context | Which role, permission, device, starting state, and interruption were present? |
| Untested dependency | What could not be reproduced without real data, configuration, integration, or hardware? |

I also stopped rescuing people immediately. Neutral clarification was different from telling them where to click. Any help was recorded, because invisible coaching could make a dependent workflow look independent.

## Training changed the meaning of a failure

I then considered what I would do if a participant's first unfamiliar task went badly.

I was tempted to reject the candidate at once. Then I noticed that I had mixed two questions:

1. Can the person discover the workflow with no preparation?
2. Can the person perform it reliably after the amount of training the salon can realistically provide?

Some tasks should be obvious without training, especially a public client booking or cancellation path. Staff workflows may reasonably require instruction. The useful distinction was whether limited training changed the result.

For a failed or assisted staff task, I would record the baseline and give the same bounded explanation the salon could actually sustain. Where practical, the retest would use an equivalent task with changed but comparable details: another service, professional, appointment time, or client state, while preserving the same underlying workflow and consequence. That made simple recall of the first route less likely to look like learning. Repeating the identical task could still be useful when no fair equivalent existed, but any improvement might reflect practice or familiarity as well as training.

That would still not prove future adoption. One retest could not reproduce a busy month, staff turnover, incentives, or the habit of returning to old tools. At most, it could show whether a comparable attempt after bounded training produced less friction, while leaving the cause of any improvement partly uncertain.

## The client test needed the client's conditions

I nearly asked a staff member to impersonate a client on the office computer.

That would have been convenient and weak.

The public booking journey includes forms, labels, instructions, validation, errors, and confirmation. W3C accessibility guidance explains why those details matter to people using screen readers, speech input, limited dexterity, or different cognitive strategies, and why accessible forms benefit users more broadly. It does not certify any salon product. ([W3C Forms Tutorial](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/forms/), updated March 27, 2026; accessed November 11, 2026.)

So the client task belonged on the type of phone and browser clients actually use, with likely clients rather than product experts. If accessibility needs were material, the test had to include them rather than simulate them through guesswork.

The trial environment created a privacy limit. Real client data could make the task more natural, but entering it into an unapproved candidate system could create a different risk. Unless the environment and handling were approved, I would use synthetic names, appointments, contact details, deposits, and notes—and record which contextual issues synthetic data might hide.

## I wrote the decision rule before watching

Without a rule, the most confident voice in the room would decide what the observations meant.

I did not invent one universal score. The tasks had different consequences.

A slow first attempt to find an internal note might be a training issue. A client unable to identify whether a cancellation succeeded could affect attendance. A receptionist creating the wrong appointment state or moving a deposit incorrectly could be materially worse than several small delays. An external paper note might preserve service for the moment while revealing that the required handoff had no usable home.

Before the test, I wrote beside each task:

- the correct observable outcome;
- whether the task was critical, important, or convenient for this salon;
- what assistance would still be acceptable after training;
- which errors would require configuration, another test, or rejection;
- which unavailable dependency had to remain an open condition in the contract or implementation plan.

The resulting decision was not simply pass or fail:

- **Proceed:** critical tasks worked under representative conditions, and remaining training/configuration needs were bounded and acceptable.
- **Retest:** a specific configuration or realistic amount of training could plausibly change the observed result.
- **Keep the risk open:** the candidate environment could not reproduce a material integration, permission, device, data, or concurrency condition.
- **Reject:** a required outcome remained unusable or unsafe under conditions the salon could not reasonably change.

This was the practical extension of [pressure-testing vendor claims](/blog/how-to-pressure-test-salon-software-vendor-claims): instead of asking only what document supports a claim, I created buyer-run evidence for the workflow itself.

## What the pre-contract test still could not prove

The test improved the decision. It did not make the future certain.

A sandbox might not contain the final permissions, migrated history, payment setup, messages, integrations, devices, or reports. A small group might behave differently from the whole team. People might return to spreadsheets after launch for reasons a short test never exposed. Real clients might encounter accessibility or account-recovery conditions the sample missed. Peak demand could change task time and error patterns.

Those were not reasons to return to the polished demo. They were reasons to label the remaining uncertainty honestly.

The question worth copying into an AI search or taking into a vendor meeting became:

<ResearchQuestion id="a20-precontract-adoption-test">How can I test whether receptionists, salon professionals, managers, and clients can complete our real booking, change, payment, and communication tasks before we sign a software contract?</ResearchQuestion>

The adoption test could show whether people could perform the work.

It could not yet show whether the reports and states created by that work were correct.

That left the next decision:

> Can the system's reports, appointment states, and financial records be proven against the underlying activity?
