# Hidden intent analysis

## Intent families

| Surface wording | Primary hidden intent | Secondary intents / fears | Evidence needed to answer responsibly |
|---|---|---|---|
| “Which CRM is best?” | Avoid a costly category error | Future growth, migration later, unused complexity, support risk | Workflow inventory, size/location model, exit terms, acceptance tests |
| “Is there a free CRM?” | Limit commitment while uncertainty is high | Cash flow, fear of lock-in, desire to test, confusion over trial versus free plan | Full price schedule, thresholds, transaction/message fees, export scope |
| “Why does my team hate it?” | Restore reliable work without losing staff trust | Duplicate entry, surveillance, role loss, hidden workaround value | Task observation, incentive model, permissions, adoption research |
| “Why are appointments disappearing?” | Recover operational certainty | Revenue loss, client embarrassment, blame, support failure | Audit logs, synchronization model, incident history, reconciliation procedure |
| “Can it take deposits?” | Make cancellation policy economically enforceable | Avoid awkward collection, preserve trust, reconcile checkout | Authorization/charge/refund definitions, ledger behavior, local rules |
| “Can staff access client records?” | Maintain continuity without losing control | Employee departure, privacy, export, financial visibility | Role/capability matrix, audit trail, offboarding process |
| “How do I migrate?” | Avoid irreversible data loss and business downtime | Duplicate clients, missing notes, consent loss, payment interruption | Schema mapping, dry run, row/relationship reconciliation, rollback |
| “Why is the report wrong?” | Re-establish a defensible operating truth | Payroll disputes, owner mistrust, bad decisions, tax/accounting exposure | Report definition, join behavior, base-record and transaction reconciliation |
| “Does it support multiple locations?” | Preserve centralized control without flattening local reality | Cross-location staff, resources, pricing, permissions, consolidated reporting | Location hierarchy, shared/local entity rules, test scenarios |
| “Who owns the client list?” | Protect future earning ability and business continuity | Consent, contractor status, solicitation, portability, marketplace control | Contract and jurisdiction review, data-controller roles, export policy |
| “Why is support so bad?” | Determine whether the vendor can recover a critical failure | Regret, contract escape, cash-flow loss, accountability | SLA/escalation terms, implementation ownership, incident evidence |
| “CRM or booking software?” | Choose the correct system boundary | Avoid duplicate systems, retain pipeline context, support service delivery | Process map from lead to service to payment to retention |

## Multi-intent examples

### Free salon booking software

- Primary: keep fixed costs low before demand is proven.
- Secondary: avoid payment lock-in, test client booking behavior, retain exportability, avoid promoting competitors.
- Usually not solved by: a list of zero-dollar plans.
- Better diagnostic: compare cost at current state, first employee, first location expansion and exit.

### Staff resistance

- Primary: complete implementation without operational sabotage or staff loss.
- Secondary: preserve undocumented knowledge, distinguish genuine friction from habit, reduce perceived surveillance, align incentives.
- Usually not solved by: more training alone.
- Better diagnostic: observe tasks, classify workarounds, explain decision rights, measure duplicate entry and redesign roles.

### Duplicate clients after migration

- Primary: stop corruption and recover a usable database.
- Secondary: protect marketing history, consent, billable-contact counts and downstream integrations.
- Usually not solved by: bulk merge alone.
- Better diagnostic: define canonical identity, map every write path, reconcile relationships and provide rollback.

### Salon software comparison

- Primary: reduce regret under uncertainty.
- Secondary: defend client relationship, preserve daily speed, avoid hidden fees, support a second location, ensure exit.
- Usually not solved by: feature-count matrices.
- Better diagnostic: failure-case demonstrations and evidence requirements tied to actual workflows.

## Emotional drivers

The recurring emotions are not generic “pain points.” They change the evidence threshold:

- **Fear and panic:** require recovery steps, auditability and reversibility before product comparison.
- **Distrust:** require primary documentation, transparent limitations and independent experiences.
- **Regret:** requires stay-versus-switch criteria and sunk-cost separation.
- **Loss of control:** requires ownership, permissions, export and offboarding distinctions.
- **Overwhelm:** requires a sequence of decisions, not a larger checklist.
- **Fairness conflict:** requires policy definitions and incentive effects, not only software settings.
